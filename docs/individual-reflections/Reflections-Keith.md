# Reflections on developing TurnUp

I owned the Organiser side of TurnUp, the WP-001 foundation and hosting work, and the WP-005 proposal-assistance feature, which turns an Organiser's PDF proposal into an editable event draft using a SoCLaaS-hosted model. When I look back over how that work actually happened, very little of my effort went into writing features myself. Most of it went into deciding what the agents should build, limiting what they were allowed to do, and judging whether their evidence really supported their claims. This reflection follows that process through spec-driven development, then multi-agent work, and finally AI security.

## Spec-Driven Development

The first real output of the project was not code but a specification set involving canonical requirements, acceptance scenarios some unresolved decision gates and a change-record template that every later increment followed. At the time the gates felt like administrative overhead. In hindsight they were the most useful part of the whole set, because they let us separate three kinds of statement that are easy to blur together: what we had agreed, what an agent had proposed, and what nobody had decided yet. That separation proved its worth as soon as hosting began. The WP-001 design researched Vercel, Neon and SoCLaaS thoroughly, yet the model, budget, data-handling and course-eligibility questions stayed open as gates. When I later chose a specific model with fixed input, output and timeout limits, the agent recorded my choice without treating it as evidence that the key had permission for that model or that any allowance remained. Because the specification kept "chosen" apart from "verified", nothing could quietly turn my preference into permission for a live call.

This shaped my view of what a good specification must make clear before implementation begins. It has to settle ownership first, because single-writer handoffs are impossible if nobody knows who owns a contract; our ownership decision named the owner of every role, shared contract and work package before any application code existed. It has to expose the difficult distinctions that a casual description hides. "Delete inactive drafts" sounds complete, but before deleting anything the agent asked me what resets the inactivity clock, how much warning an Organiser should receive, and whether a previously published event should be deleted or restored. My answers, a thirty-day window reset only by saving a draft or accepting a PDF extraction, an in-app warning from day twenty-three, and restoration rather than deletion for events that had once been published, became the actual contract. A good specification also uses numbers rather than adjectives. The daily generation policy was buildable because it said ten shared attempts, 4,096 output tokens per request, 40,960 reserved tokens per day, a reset at midnight Singapore time and dispatched failures counting against the limit, rather than asking for "a reasonable daily limit". Finally, it has to say what is still open, since an unresolved gate written down is far less dangerous than one an agent fills in silently.

The most reliable way I found to tell whether a spec was precise enough for an agent was to watch whether the agent had to stop and ask me something, or whether it made a choice I later had to undo. Both happened, and the contrast was instructive. Prefilling event dates from a PDF needed a rule for times without a stated zone, and I only settled that rule partway through implementation: complete unzoned dates default to Singapore time, explicit zones win, and incomplete or ambiguous dates stay unresolved. Independent review then found that explicit "Singapore" wording had been handled under the rule I had just replaced. The agent had not behaved unreasonably; my specification had shifted underneath it. The clarification feature went the other way. I gave nine explicit decisions before any code was written, covering where issues appear, how confirmation works, what blocks preview, when answers are saved and what happens when a PDF is removed or regenerated, and that increment needed far fewer corrections. My working test now is whether another implementer could predict the important outcomes, including failure, staleness and repetition, without inventing a rule on my behalf. If they would have to guess, the spec is not ready. Review findings sometimes pointed at the specification rather than the code, which was a useful signal in itself. In the issue-tracking skill, a reviewer noticed that one step told the agent to follow our original ownership decision permanently, even though the shared guidance said later human decisions take precedence. The defect lay in the instruction, not in anything the agent had produced.

Changing requirements taught me that a change is not one edit but a chain that runs from the human decision through the canonical spec, the implementation and the tests, and finally into the evidence. When I decided that human review should be recorded on GitHub pull requests rather than through local sign-off rows, that single decision touched twenty files, and the reviewer checked that the product specifications and scenarios stayed byte-identical while the review policy around them changed. When I accepted a new rescheduling rule for event publication, the canonical text recorded my decision without claiming Darryl's agreement on the parts that affected his consumer work, so my change could not be mistaken for a shared one. I also added a reconciliation procedure that carries a finalised decision into the affected GitHub issues, so that tracking did not drift away from the specification. The chain must also include the claim of verification. One design record had been marked reviewed and complete when a later commit changed the design outside the reviewed snapshot, and the honest response was to narrow the completion claim back to what had actually been reviewed until a fresh audit covered the new version.

The question of whether tests demonstrate the intent of a spec, rather than merely its wording, is where I learned the most, mainly from tests that passed while the behaviour was wrong. The hosted SoCLaaS probe initially counted any successful adapter response as success, but the intent was that the model had genuinely answered, so the correction required an exact sentinel value and treated anything else as an invalid response. The Vercel deployment workflow passed every local check and then failed three separate times after merging, on the package install, on capturing the deployment URL and on authenticating the smoke check; each fix added a regression test that failed against the original behaviour, yet each record still acknowledged that only a real hosted run could prove the deployed fix. Hosted activation of PDF extraction later failed before ever reaching the provider, because the full source test suite could not see a transformation that only occurred in the production bundle, and the fix was a build-artifact regression rather than any relaxation of validation. The publication approval hash passed its unit tests but would have rejected valid approvals once the database reordered object keys, and that only surfaced when the reviewer ran the real hashing functions against a real database round trip. A concurrency test that passed on macOS failed on real Windows CI, and the fix deliberately left the original test unchanged.

These experiences made me more careful about what kind of evidence a test actually gives. A mocked provider can show that my prompt is assembled correctly and that my processor handles a response, but it cannot show that the model will produce a useful answer. A reviewer once flagged a synthetic evidence fixture that passed every check while being semantically weak enough that it would not have convinced a person. When I reformatted the prompt source for readability, the test that mattered was not that the suite still passed but a byte-for-byte comparison of the assembled prompts before and after. Tests reflect the intent of a spec when they start from a failure a user would notice and work backwards, rather than restating the implementation's own assumptions.

## Basic Multi-Agent Software Engineering

I began with four setup specialists, a coordinator, a requirements analyst, a repository builder and an independent specification reviewer, and for WP-001 I added a foundation engineer, a hosting engineer, an implementation verifier and a delivery agent, each configured with its own model and reasoning level. Not all of these earned their cost equally. The role that clearly did was the independent verifier, whose findings were concrete and frequently security-relevant. It found that duplicate TLS parameters could silently disable certificate verification for the database driver and that a query-string host override could redirect an otherwise validated database URL. It found that destructive gate tests were reachable through an ordinary test command, that a correction for Vercel account scoping rejected a legitimate account format, and that a regular expression in the injection safeguards could block the server for about thirty-five seconds on a crafted page. Parallel read-only research also worked well: the foundation and hosting engineers investigated official sources side by side, and only the requirements analyst wrote to the canonical specification afterwards. Other roles mattered less than I expected. The main agent often acted as coordinator itself, and some work was simply too small to justify a team, so documentation-only increments went without a verifier, later merges were done by a single agent, and the injection safeguards were written by one agent with one separate reviewer. I had initially confused a sequence of activities, analysing then building then testing, with a useful division of labour. Separate agents paid off where independence or genuine parallelism was the point, which in practice meant review, research and work on disjoint files.

Building and testing the specialists well turned out to be harder than defining them. My early testing was almost entirely structural: every agent definition parsed, the model settings matched and the skill links resolved, but no agent had been launched or tried on a real task, and even the bundled skill validator could not run because a dependency was missing. A well-formed definition says very little about how an agent behaves. Better evidence came from using the skills on real but reversible work. The issue-tracking skill was dry-run on three draft issues before anything was published, including a review of the titles alone to see whether they made sense without their bodies, and its ownership rule was checked against specific cases such as whether a merely proposed change could override an accepted one. I also built an offline usage tracker so I could see what the agents actually cost for each change. If I did this again, I would forward-test each specialist on a small task seeded with a known defect and judge it by whether it found the defect, not by how thorough its role description sounded.

Passing context between agents is where important qualifications are most easily lost. The handoffs that worked named the exact files each agent could write, so that only one writer ever touched a file and integration points were explicit. They pinned the precise revision or fingerprint that had been reviewed, so that delivery could confirm the committed code was the same code that had been approved and an "approved" label could not drift onto something different. Above all, they carried forward what had not been checked and why, whether that was a live database never reached because credentials were missing, a reviewer's database test that timed out, or a native browser check that could not run. The phrase "offline tests passed but the database check never ran" is exactly the kind of qualification that disappears when one agent summarises another. The best handoffs ended with a written contract for whoever came next; the clarification work closed with a document describing the request and save contract, the canonical values, the revision behaviour, the separation of private and public data and the migration steps, and the foundation handoff told Darryl precisely which database capabilities were ready for his integration and which decisions still blocked him.

When one agent writes the spec, another implements it and a third reviews it, my records show errors slipping through at every seam. Between specification and implementation, a superseded rule was still applied, as with the time-zone case, and the deployment workflow accepted nested preview branches while the documented environment policy only matched single-segment ones. Between implementation and review, a reviewer could approve a snapshot that later changed, or inherit the author's blind spots by relying on the author's summary instead of the diff, and usage-limit interruptions cut some reviews off midway so that I had to record what had genuinely been completed after resuming. Between review and delivery, a delivery agent reported that the remote branch matched while a separate verifier could not even reach GitHub to confirm it, and the record kept those two claims apart rather than merging them into one. Malicious instructions are a different kind of risk. Our development fixtures deliberately contained text asking agents to read private files or create marker files, and verifiers were checked to make sure they did neither. Any text an agent reads, whether a fixture, a PDF or another agent's report, can contain instructions, and only the human assignment carries authority. The defence was the same in each case: the reviewer reads the actual diff and requirements, reruns the checks itself, and keeps evidence the author produced separate from evidence it reproduced independently.

Each change therefore left a verification record listing who ran each check, on what scope, with the exact command, the result and its limitations, alongside every finding with its severity, consequence, correction and recheck. Each prompt left a log entry recording what I asked for and what happened, and anything written to GitHub was read back independently and compared with what had been intended. The most valuable entries were the negative ones, such as a check that was never run, a build that failed because the disk was full, or a final build of the corrected source that had not yet passed. A person can only verify an agent's work if the record shows what was skipped as clearly as what passed. The weakness of this approach is volume. Some of my later records became so dense that they were hard for a human to read, which undermines the very purpose of leaving evidence.

## AI Security

The main attack surface in my area was the proposal PDF, an untrusted document fed into a model whose output becomes event fields that Students will later trust. In planning the safeguards I considered direct injection, where the proposal openly instructs the assistant; obfuscated injection, using invisible characters, bidirectional controls, Unicode tag characters and disguised punctuation in links; Best-of-N attempts, where many paraphrased variants are tried in the hope that one succeeds; and indirect injection hidden in the PDF itself, such as text smaller than a point or placed outside the visible page so that a human reviewer would never see it. Other surfaces were less obvious. The model's output is itself an attack surface, since an injected link or contact detail could be quoted back as "evidence" and prefilled into a real event. Public FAQ generation had to be restricted to saved, resolved public fields so that private proposal material could never leak into public answers. The development process was exposed too, through fixtures aimed at the coding agents, and so was the infrastructure, where a preview workflow could have claimed a production hostname, database connection strings could be manipulated to weaken TLS or redirect the host, and crafted input could exhaust either a regular expression or the provider quota.

The safeguards I implemented are layered so that no single layer depends on the model behaving well. The parser normalises text, strips invisible and control characters, and discards text that is too small or lies outside the page. The prompts place a shared block of security rules first, mark the PDF as data and repeat a reminder after it. The server then requires every extracted value to cite an exact quote from the page, rejects quotes taken from instruction-like sentences, checks that any contact detail actually appears in the source, and enforces a strict response schema. Pages containing assistant-directed instructions receive a deterministic note asking the Organiser to check values drawn from them, and nothing is saved, previewed or published without the Organiser's explicit action. When something fails, the client receives a generic message while the server logs only a fixed rejection code, never the raw model output. Testing exposed real gaps in this design. One test showed that checking contact details against the page was not enough on its own, because the model could quote the injected sentence and so make the injected link look supported, which is why rejection of instruction-like quotes was added. The reviewer's adversarial probing then found the catastrophic regex backtracking, instruction markers so broad that they suppressed ordinary sentences such as "you are now registered", and a review note that promised more than it delivered, and the fixes themselves introduced three new issues that a recheck caught. The live injection walkthrough against the real model is documented but has not yet been run, and the specification openly lists the forms that remain undetected, such as an injection split across sentences. A finite test suite cannot prove the absence of injection. I have come to think about security less in terms of whether the model ignores an attack and more in terms of what an influenced model would still be allowed to cause.

That thinking guided the limits on both kinds of agent in the project. The product's model was constrained in code and in the database rather than in its prompt: a durable admission gate allowed one request at a time with a minimum interval between calls, each call had a fixed output cap, cumulative attempts and tokens were capped, and later each Organiser received a daily allowance that reset at midnight with dispatched failures still counting. Hosted generation was disabled by default and remains disabled in Production. The development agents were bounded by their assignments. They could write only to named files, worked only against synthetic non-production databases with no access to private Production data, and never saw secrets, which I entered myself rather than pasting into chat. Installing an agent definition granted no authority to implement, commit, push or deploy, and live work came with explicit caps, such as permission for at most two hosted inference calls during activation, of which exactly two were used. The tooling enforced some boundaries independently; automated attempts to list every database project and create a branch were refused until I approved the specific named resources.

Human approval sat at each point where an action was hard to reverse or spent something real. Plans were approved before implementation, GitHub issues were created only after I approved the drafts, test databases were deleted only after a fresh confirmation, every refill of the model allowance happened only on my explicit request and was followed by an independent read-only check, and commits, pushes and deployments happened only when a prompt authorised them, with human review taking place on the pull request. Three practices kept these gates from being compromised. Authority came only from my requests and never from content, so instructions inside fixtures, PDFs or another agent's summary were treated as data. Agents were explicitly forbidden from inferring or claiming that human review or approval had occurred. And each approval was scoped, so permission for a local testing allowance did not extend to a hosted budget, and approving a preview deployment did not touch Production.

The gates were not perfect, and the failures were instructive. During hosted readiness work, an unsupported command-line invocation unexpectedly created an empty Vercel project and attempted an upload before failing. The agent stopped using that tool, confirmed through the API that the project had no deployments, environment variables, repository link or domains, deleted only that project, and recorded that it could not establish exactly what had been uploaded. Similarly, when the first hosted activation failed, the agent disabled the feature and rolled back rather than retrying. Approval gates reduce risk, but they cannot stop a tool from doing something unexpected inside an approved step. What protected the project in those moments was narrow scope, synthetic data, and an agent that reported the incident plainly rather than hiding it.

Across all three areas, my role shifted from generating more AI activity to deciding what authority to grant and what evidence to trust. I would keep the decision gates, the single-writer handoffs and the independent review. Next time, though, I would start with fewer agents, test them against deliberately planted defects before relying on them, and keep the records short enough that a person will actually read them.
